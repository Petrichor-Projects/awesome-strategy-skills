# Nine Strategy Skills, One Positioning Decision

Nine published skills received the same fictional evidence pack. The pack described a $24 million B2B SaaS company whose 2023 “AI copilot” position no longer matched buyer language, competitive pressure, or the controls already present in its product.

The benchmark produced a less convenient finding than a leaderboard: **artifact type predicts what the skill leaves unresolved.**

## The result

| Rank on this task | Skill | Score | Native artifact |
|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Relevancy Audit | 23 / 24 | Positioning-decay diagnosis |
| 2 | One Page Strategy | 22 / 24 | Facts–Problem–Idea–Solution strategy |
| 3 | Competitor Profiling | 19 / 24 | Source-traceable competitive profile |
| 3 | Scenario Planning | 19 / 24 | Scenarios, options, and triggers |
| 5 | Customer Research | 18 / 24 | Evidence synthesis and research gaps |
| 6 | Competitive Intelligence | 17 / 24 | Cross-functional positioning update |
| 7 | Product Marketing Context | 16 / 24 | Shared downstream context |
| 8 | Positioning Craft | 15 / 24 | Positioning canvas |
| 9 | Strategy Frameworks | 13 / 24 | Where-to-play/how-to-win strategy |

These are task-fit scores, not permanent grades. The complete [method](METHOD.md), [outputs](outputs/), and [dimension evidence](SCORES.md) are public.

## Finding 1: A decision gate beats fluent completeness

The strongest outputs made a commitment and named what would reverse it. Relevancy Audit scored 23 because the native workflow asks whether the position should be preserved, refreshed, or replaced, then ties that decision to evidence, owners, claim limits, and review triggers.

One Page Strategy reached 22 through a different route. Its compact four-part structure forces the problem, belief, action, and exclusion into a form a leadership team can repeat. Its inversion questions made falsifiability and disagreement part of the artifact.

The two lowest-scoring outputs were not incoherent. They left more judgment outside the workflow. A positioning canvas can express a choice without governing it. A generic strategy template can name a moat without proving one exists.

## Finding 2: Research skills protected truth better than they made choices

Competitor Profiling and Customer Research were the strictest external workflows on source quality. They preserved missing data, sample limits, raw evidence, recency, and confidence. That behavior matters when a company is tempted to treat a few vivid quotes as a market.

They did not own the final commitment. Their native job is to improve the evidence base. Using either as the last step would leave the preserve-refresh-replace decision with the operator.

That is not a defect. It is an interface boundary.

## Finding 3: Uncertainty became useful when it had an owner

Scenario Planning did not ask which future was most likely. It separated moves that made sense in every future from contingent moves and small options. More important, it assigned each early-warning indicator an owner and review cadence.

Competitive Intelligence produced similar operating value through a recurring system: monthly tracking, triggered responses, audience-specific outputs, and named cross-functional consumers. Its weaker point was provenance. It tells an operator what to track, but not with the same raw-source and confidence discipline as the research skills.

## Finding 4: Safety is still mostly implicit

No skill fabricated a fact or made an unsafe external move in this run. Only Competitor Profiling directly treated external pages as untrusted input. Several skills preserved human approval. Few combined data boundaries, permission limits, claim review, and external-action controls throughout the workflow.

This was the lowest common design maturity across an otherwise useful set.

## What HelioDesk should do

All nine artifacts point away from preserving the current position unchanged. The defensible decision is a reversible replacement test:

1. Test a controlled, defensible resolution frame against the current position and a refresh-only alternative.
2. Preserve deployment speed and ticket deflection as continuity proof for the installed base.
3. Separate generally available controls from beta and internally validated evidence.
4. Add risk and operations stakeholders, ResolveGrid, and governance criteria to qualification.
5. Commit, narrow, or revert at day 60 using buyer comprehension, live-opportunity progression, proof objections, and installed-base confusion.

The evidence supports a direction. It does not prove a final category name.

## The practical stack

The benchmark suggests a sequence, not a winner-takes-all tool:

1. **Customer Research** establishes the buyer signal and its limits.
2. **Competitor Profiling** establishes comparable market evidence.
3. **Relevancy Audit** decides whether the current position survives.
4. **Scenario Planning** tests the decision against unresolved futures.
5. **One Page Strategy** makes the choice repeatable.
6. **Positioning Craft** expresses the market frame.
7. **Product Marketing Context** distributes the approved truth to downstream teams.
8. **Competitive Intelligence** keeps the system current.

## What this benchmark does not prove

- It does not measure model quality across repeated runs.
- It does not compare cost, latency, or usability with live teams.
- It does not show that a 23-point skill creates better company outcomes.
- It does not establish independent validation. Petrichor created the highest-scoring skill and scored the benchmark.

The next credible step is blind outside review, then a second fixture in a different decision domain. Until those exist, the scores are evidence records, not authority theater.
